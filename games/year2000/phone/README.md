# THE PHONE OF THE FUTURE (electronics, rev A)

A solderable toy kit that goes with In The Year 2000!: "a real working wireless phone", the
way a 1993 game imagined one. Infrared voice and touch tones, an eight-digit red display, a
real pulse dial, a PIC16F84A and an MT8870, through-hole parts only.

**This is an untested design. Nobody has built one. Treat every claim below as "should", not "does".**
Only the firmware's logic has been exercised (in a simulator I wrote), and the schematic and board
pass KiCad's electrical and design-rule checks. Nothing was powered, soldered or listened to.

This folder is the electronics only. A later task designs the 3D-printed shell and dial from
`mechanical/interface.json`.

## Theory of operation

One infrared beam carries both live voice and touch-tone digits, half duplex.

- **Sending voice.** Hold TALK. TALK connects +5 V to the microphone's bias resistor, so the electret is powered
  only while the button is down (no room noise goes out otherwise). A half of the LM358 amplifies it (gain 47) around a
  half-supply bias, and the signal modulates a 2N3904 that drives the 940 nm IR LED around a steady bias current of about 9 mA.
- **Sending digits.** Dialing a digit makes the PIC play the real DTMF tone pair for about 103 ms. Two pins output square
  waves at the row and column frequencies (697/770/852/941 Hz and 1209/1336/1477/1633 Hz, ITU-T Q.23). Two 10 k resistors and
  10 nF sum and soften them; the sum goes to the IR driver and, through a large resistor, to the speaker, so the sending phone
  hears its own tones too.
- **Receiving.** A PIN photodiode with a 22 k load, AC-coupled into the other LM358 half (gain 47, rolled off above ~7 kHz), gives
  the audio. An LM386 (gain 20) plays it on the speaker. The same audio goes to the MT8870, which turns tone pairs into a 4-bit
  code (Q1..Q4) and raises StD.
- **No hearing yourself.** While TALK is held, a transistor shorts the speaker amplifier's input (the receiver is muted) and
  the firmware ignores the decoder.
- **Display.** The MAX7219 scans two 4-digit common-cathode modules. The PIC writes it by bit-banging three wires. A digit
  typed or received is added at the right of the number, left-aligned; the left digit's decimal point lights while TALK is held;
  an empty display shows a dash at the left. There is no call set-up: digits just appear on both phones.
- **Dial.** SW2 (pulse) closes once per number step during the return; SW3 (off-normal) is closed whenever the dial is off rest. The PIC
  counts closures after SW3 closes (debounce about 1 ms for pulses, 15 ms for off-normal, so a fast printed dial with no
  governor still counts), and when SW3 opens the count is the digit; 10 pulses are 0, more than 10 are ignored.
- **CLEAR / hang up** is the PIC's reset button (MCLR): it clears the number.
- **Clock.** One 3.579545 MHz crystal runs the MT8870; the PIC (XT mode) takes the clock from the MT8870's OSC2 through a
  30 pF capacitor, as the MT8870 datasheet does when chaining decoders. The PIC runs at 894,886 instructions per second, and the tone loop is exactly
  24 cycles (37.3 kHz sampling).

### Pin budget (PIC16F84A, 13 I/O)

| Pin | Use | | Pin | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RA0 | TALK (high = held) | | RB0 | dial pulse (low = closed, weak pull-up) |
| RA1 | MAX7219 DIN | | RB1 | dial off-normal (low = closed, weak pull-up) |
| RA2 | MAX7219 CLK | | RB2 | tone square wave A |
| RA3 | MAX7219 LOAD | | RB3 | tone square wave B |
| RA4 | MT8870 StD (input) | | RB4..RB7 | MT8870 Q1..Q4 |

All 13 are used. CLEAR takes none because it is MCLR; the receiver mute and the mic gating take none because TALK drives them in hardware.

### Power: four AAA cells and a 5 V low-dropout regulator

Voltage limits: MAX7219 4.0 to 5.5 V; PIC16F84A 2.0 to 5.5 V; MT8870D 4.75 to 5.25 V recommended (6.0 V absolute); LM386-1 4 to 12 V;
LM358 3 to 32 V. Three cells (4.5 V fresh) fall under the MAX7219's 4.0 V within the first half of their life, and four fresh alkaline
cells (6.4 V) are over the MT8870's limit, so: **four AAA cells into an LM2940 5.0 V regulator** (0.5 V maximum dropout, 1 A, so it also feeds the speaker amplifier
and display peaks). Expected behavior (calculated, not measured): a steady 5 V until the cells average about 1.3 V each; below that the
MT8870 may start to miss digits and below about 1.05 V per cell the display goes. The slide switch is in series with the battery. Estimated draw is 70 to 100 mA
with eight digits lit and the IR bias on, so roughly 10 hours from alkaline AAAs. A pulse-limited IR LED bias would help; it is left as is for simplicity.

## Build order for a beginner

You need a 25 to 40 W iron, thin rosin-core solder, flush cutters, a multimeter, and four AAA cells. Use sockets for the four ICs.
Parts on the back (the battery side) go in first and are soldered from the front; the front parts (display, buttons, switches, LED,
photodiode) go in last and are soldered from the back. Test at each step.

1. Check the board against the Gerbers (`out/phone-gerbers.zip` view in any Gerber viewer) when it arrives; check no two pads are bridged.
2. Back side, power only: battery holder, slide switch, LM2940 (lying flat), C1 and C2 (tantalum: mind the + mark). **Fit no ICs.**
   Put cells in, switch on: the multimeter must read 5.0 V (+/-0.1) between the regulator's pins 3 and 2. Do not continue if not.
3. Sockets, then the resistors and capacitors (shortest first). The electrolytics have polarity: the square pad is +.
4. The crystal, Q1 and Q2 (flat face matches the silkscreen), then the ICs with the power off. Pin 1 is the square pad.
5. Front: the MAX7219 display modules. Power up: the display should show a dash at the left. If it is blank, check ISET (R26) and the 5 V.
6. The TALK and CLEAR buttons, the two microswitches. Press CLEAR: it should show the dash. Hand-rock SW3 and SW2 in sequence to
   mimic a dial (SW3 closed, then SW2 tapped 3 times, then SW3 open): a 3 should appear.
7. Speaker and microphone (wires from the pads). A dialed digit now makes a tone from the speaker.
8. IR LED and photodiode last (bend leads 90 degrees so they face the top edge, about 1 mm off the board). Point two phones at each other a hand's length apart
   and dial: the digit should appear on both. Hold TALK on one and speak: expect a quiet, noisy voice on the other. Tune by ear (see below).

Likely first things to adjust: R19 (receive gain), R20 and R16 (speaker level), R12 and R15 (IR modulation depth), R9 (IR bias), R26 (display brightness).

| Check | Result |
| --- | --- |
| KiCad ERC (`tools/export.sh`) | 0 violations |
| KiCad DRC | 0 violations and 0 unconnected, with three rules set to *ignore* in `kicad/phone.kicad_pro`: `silk_over_copper` and `silk_overlap` (silkscreen text sits near pads; fabs clip it) and `lib_footprint_mismatch` (back parts are mirrored copies of the library footprints) |
| Schematic vs board parity (`--schematic-parity`) | not run |
| gpasm assembly | assembles (`tools/build_firmware.sh`) |
| Firmware simulation | passes `tools/picsim.py`, my own PIC16F84A instruction simulator: start-up, a dialed digit at 33 ms and at 8 ms pulses, all ten digits' tone pairs (Goertzel), 10 pulses = 0, 11 ignored, received digits, TALK mutes and lights the point |
| Not simulated | timing against the real MT8870 (its tone length and guard time), the analog parts, the IR link's range, the LM2940's behavior, anything on a breadboard; gpsim was installed but not used |
| Gerbers and drill | exported by kicad-cli; not opened in a viewer or sent to a board house |
| Parts stock | see `PARTS.md`; very little was looked up (the MT8870 DIP is listed obsolete at Digi-Key) |

The board has no ground pour (I could not fill zones without KiCad's Python, and chose not to run it), so ground is routed as tracks.
Expect more hum and noise than a poured board would have.

## Assumptions to confirm before ordering

- **Display module**: 0.36 in 4-digit common-cathode, 12 pins, "5641AS-style" pinout (1 E, 2 D, 3 DP, 4 C, 5 G, 6 D4, 7 B, 8 D3, 9 D2, 10 F, 11 A, 12 D1), 10.16 mm between pin rows, 30 x 14 mm body.
- **Microswitch** pins (5.08 mm apart), **battery holder** pad positions, **slide switch** pitch, **tactile** pin spacing: placeholders, see `tools/footprints.py` (marked ASSUMED).
- The **MT8870 OSC2 to PIC OSC1** link through 30 pF is modeled on the MT8870 datasheet's chained-decoder circuit; it has not been tried with a PIC.
- Component heights in the mechanical file are typical values.
