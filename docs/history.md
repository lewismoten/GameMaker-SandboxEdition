# Lewis and Game-Maker

I'm Lewis Moten ("Lewie"), and I keep Game-Maker: Sandbox Edition. I was one of the
original buyers of RSD Game-Maker in 1992, when it was sold for DOS PCs as a way to make
your own games without programming. This page is how I came to it, and how that shows up
here.

## Fort Ashby, 1990

My family moved to Fort Ashby, in Mineral County, West Virginia, in November 1990, during
the county's hunting-week break from school. Before the move I had been at Eastern
Vocational Technical High School in Baltimore; in West Virginia I went to Frankfort High
School. Our house was on a hill. My brother bought an IBM PS/2, a 286, with Mom's help, and
I used it often.

I made friends at a video rental store in town that had a pool table, and the arcade
machines were further into town, upstairs at a place across from the convenience store. I
painted ceramics at the ceramic shop, and I was a junior firefighter at the volunteer fire
department on the corner: I listened to the fire radios, dried and rolled hoses, rode the
brush truck once, and dropped suction hoses into the river at the fairgrounds during night
drills. We flew model rockets at the fairgrounds too.

Across from the grocery store was a little library, one big eight-sided room and a copier,
and next to it a hardware store where I bought bits for my router and scroll saw for
woodworking. There was a pizza shop at the bottom of the hill where I lived, and a friend
who also had a computer, where we played games.

## Moten Snow to Software Operations, 1992

In October 1992, _Computer Gaming World_ #99 advertised RSD Game-Maker for $89 plus $4
shipping. The next issue listed it at $129. I was 16.

On December 11, 1992, a nor'easter snowed the area in (the _Mineral Daily News-Tribune_
ran "Keyser area is snowbound" that day), and the snow became my first business. From
December 1992 to February 1993 I shoveled driveways around Fort Ashby: finding customers,
setting prices, scheduling the work and doing it well in the cold. When I had the money I
bought a money order and sent it off for Game-Maker.

Once the box arrived I went from moving snow to making software. I built tile-based
adventure games and learned how game logic works. Its sound editor stuck with me most: you
drew a sound's wave with the mouse, and a few bytes became an 8-bit sound effect.

My copy wouldn't start a game without its title card, `GMTITLE.GIF`: RSD's "Game-Maker
Presents:" picture, shown before every game. Plenty of authors drew their own card in its place;
I just left RSD's. (Game-Maker 3.00's player stopped asking for it.)

I graduated in May 1994, and my parents gave me a 486 with a CD-ROM drive as a graduation
gift. I left for Pittsburgh in June.

## The author, on Usenet, 1992

A month after the October ad, the program's author answered a stranger's question online. On
November 18, 1992, in the Usenet group rec.games.programmer, Stacy Purcell asked whether
anyone had used Recreational Software's Game-Maker. Andy Stone replied, said
up front that he wrote it, and described it as a way for people with no programming
background to make action-adventure, role-playing and shoot-'em-up games in 256-color VGA,
with smoothly scrolling scenes and a utility for pulling pictures out of GIF files. He was
plain about its limits: it was not meant for long professional games, but it worked well for
shareware and for prototypes. He gave the price as about $89 from KD Software. Two days
later Kirk Baum answered that he would like to see examples before paying. Both posts survive
in a 1992 Usenet collection on Discmaster
([Stone](https://discmaster.textfiles.com/view/28245/NN_1992_27.iso/spool/rec/games/programm/4743),
[Baum](https://discmaster.textfiles.com/view/28245/NN_1992_27.iso/spool/rec/games/programm/4778)).

## Other published accounts

These entries are dated by publication. Later recollections are attributed to their
speakers rather than treated as contemporary release records. Each source has a checked
Wayback snapshot; the snapshot dates below are in UTC.

- **May 14, 2013 — The Exchange and the box pictures.** In EJR Tairne's interview
  outtakes, Andy Stone says he kept user letters from the Game-Maker Exchange but no
  submitted disks. He also explains that some scenes pictured on the packaging were
  experiments, including an isometric maze that never became a Nebula level. This helps
  distinguish evidence of user submissions and development experiments from surviving,
  released games.
  [Original: “Bonus Time with Andy Stone”](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/bonus-time-with-andy-stone);
  [Wayback: June 5, 2025](https://web.archive.org/web/20250605234636/https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/bonus-time-with-andy-stone).
- **May 16, 2013 — Revised games and shareware catalogs.** In another Tairne interview,
  Gary Acord describes revising older games around 2010 with his modified RSD Game-Maker
  3.0, slowing enemies and expanding character movement. He names Big Byte, JCSM Public
  Software Library and Mom And Pop's Software as distributors. His account documents a
  route to players through shareware catalogs and explains why later copies should not
  automatically be treated as the original builds.
  [Original: “Inside the Mind of Gary Acord”](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/inside-the-mind-of-gary-acord);
  [Wayback: June 5, 2025](https://web.archive.org/web/20250605234652/https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/inside-the-mind-of-gary-acord).
- **October 11, 2017 — A magazine reader becomes a game maker.** Stacy Davidson recalls
  ordering the DOS Game-Maker after discovering it in a magazine, then using it to put
  graphics and sound into a game world. This is an independent user's account of how
  magazine exposure led to making games. It does not identify the issue, purchase date or
  Game-Maker version.
  [Original: “Learn or die”](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/learn-or-die);
  [Wayback: August 7, 2024](https://web.archive.org/web/20240807004629/https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/learn-or-die).
- **April 16, 2019 — PPP Team's games and shared artwork.** In his French memoir,
  Cyborg Jeff recalls discovering RSD Game-Maker in late 1994. He describes early projects,
  including Pengo's Adventure, Panzer 1945 and Badman, using the supplied artwork before
  PPP Team made more of its own scenery and monsters. This adds a participant's account
  of how the bundled materials helped a game-making group get started; the 1994 date is
  his recollection.
  [Original: “Notre rêve : faire des jeux vidéo”](https://www.lesmondesdecyborgjeff.be/2019/04/16/notre-reve-faire-des-jeux-video/);
  [Wayback: May 12, 2025](https://web.archive.org/web/20250512212945/https://www.lesmondesdecyborgjeff.be/2019/04/16/notre-reve-faire-des-jeux-video/).

## Later

That sound editor kept coming back in my own projects:

- [Creating a UFO game](https://lewismoten.com/2024/04/15/creating-a-ufo-game/): a game I
  began in Second Life in 2009, where you hunt for aliens with a flashlight, and brought to
  the web with drawn sound effects in the spirit of Game-Maker's.
- [Data over audio](https://lewismoten.com/2024/05/01/data-over-audio/): sending data
  between two devices as sound, from memories of cassette drives and a 2400 baud modem
  that tied up the phone line.
- [Game design](https://lewismoten.com/2024/05/20/game-design/): plans for a GPS scavenger
  hunt, with sound effects made from 36 bytes of data, much as Game-Maker's editor did.

I also built a web page that lets you draw a sound effect with the mouse, the way
Game-Maker's sound designer did.

## Where it shows up in this edition

### Snowbound: The $89 Shovel

_Snowbound_ is that winter as a game. It is set in Fort Ashby in December 1992. A kid has a
shovel, a coffee can and a plan. He has to clear the neighborhood's driveways at a dollar a
scoop, sled down Jamie Street, and get $89 to LaVale Mall before the price of Game-Maker
goes up. The streets are the ones I grew up on; the story is that year, told as a game made
with the program it is about.

### The 1994 desk

The desk recreates how games like these were found and shared in the early 1990s, before
most homes had the internet:

- **A PC on a desk**, switched on with a power button, booting to a DOS prompt. Game-Maker
  runs there with its own menus and designers, its copy check asking for a word from the
  Owner's Guide, and a registration card to mail.
- **A modem and a phone line.** TERMINAL dials the bulletin board systems (BBSes) of the
  412 area code, Pittsburgh's. You pick the modem's speed, from 300 baud to 56 kbps, and
  text and files arrive as slowly as they did. Somebody picking up the kitchen phone drops
  the call.
- **The BBSes**, where shareware games were found: file areas and upload/download ratios,
  bulletins from the SysOp, a picture gallery, a teleconference, a door game played one
  turn per call, and the friendly regulars of The Sofa.
- **The boxes on the shelf**, with their manuals, maps, hint books and stickers, the way
  games came home from the store.
- **The room around it**: the wall phone and the numbers taped beside it, a wristwatch
  clock, a black-light poster, a window that turns to night.

The point is the feeling of it: waiting on a download, trading games on a board, and
finding out what a box of software could let a kid make.

### The sound designer

Game-Maker's sound designer is here too, in the desk's DOS Game-Maker and in today's web
editor: draw the wave, hear the effect.

## Today

Andy Stone released Game-Maker's source code under the MIT License in 2014. This fork turns
that source into a version that runs in a web browser, keeps the original file formats, and
adds new games made with it. See the [README](../README.md) for what is in it.
