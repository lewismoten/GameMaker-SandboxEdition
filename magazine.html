The Design Desk

You’re making games, and your friends are getting stuck in them. Here are four from this month’s stack, and what we would change.

Exit, stage nowhere

My cousin played my castle game for an hour, then quit at the big sign that says EXIT. He walked into it about forty times. It isn’t the way out, I only painted it there because castles have signs. Now he tells everybody my game is broken.

— SIR LANCELOT, Cumberland, Md.

TB: Your cousin has a point. A sign that says EXIT is a promise, and in Game-Maker a link has no picture of its own: it’s just a spot on the map, so players only know a way out by what you draw there. Paint over the sign, or move the real link under it. Then draw every real way out the same way, and that sign will mean something.

No left turn

My knight has a sword key for every direction. Q swings right, W swings up, E swings down, and I ran out of good keys, so left is on the semicolon. Every tester says the knight can’t fight anything on his left.

— SQUIRE, Frostburg, Md.

TB: One key will do it. A swipe the knight fires flies the way he last moved and turns to face that way, so draw it facing right, put it on the Space bar, and it swings left when he walks left. That frees Q, W and E for something fun.

Cloudy with a chance of falling

I keep falling through the clouds in my big brother’s sky level. Some hold you up and some don’t, and they look exactly the same. He says that’s the challenge. I say it’s mean.

— SKYDIVER, Keyser, W.Va.

TB: We’re with you. Two blocks with the same picture can have different solid sides, which is how secret doors work, but a secret is fair only when something gives it away. Tell your brother to give the clouds you can stand on a firm, bright top edge, and the ones you can’t a thin, gray, rainy look.

Round and round

My desert level is one long road, and the map wraps, so it goes on forever. My dad drove it for twenty minutes before he asked me where the oasis was. It was behind him the whole time.

— DUNE BUGGY, Butler, Pa.

TB: A road that loops looks the same on every lap, so nobody can tell they’re going round. Plant something one of a kind near the seam, a cactus wearing a hat will do, and a sign that points to the oasis. Then let your dad off at the next rest stop.
Music, but no sound effects?

FM music and digitized sound use different parts of a Sound Blaster. Lewis’s PC had IRQ 7 on both sides; the culprit was sound/music set to None, not an IRQ mismatch. Select your sound hardware in CONFIG. The usual example is 220 / IRQ 7 / DMA 1.

Seven is not a magic number! Check your card’s setup utility or jumper settings, then make the game’s IRQ match the card’s actual IRQ.

The fix in one line

Draw a way out at every link, and nowhere else.

One fire key fires every way.

Floors look like floors; scenery looks like scenery.

More of what players gripe about, and how to fix it, is in this issue’s Design Workshop.

Why does Escape restart my level?

A quick tap ends the scene, but Game-Maker quits only if Escape is still held after the fade. Let go too soon and the level can start over. On a slow computer, hold Escape until the screen goes black, then release it. Give that pinkie a little patience.

Garbage at the bottom of the screen?

Stray stripes under the playfield? Check for the wrong SVGA type before repainting your level. Run CONFIG, then choose your card or Simulate in the video settings. Save the choice and start the game again. A card-specific driver is no help when it belongs to somebody else’s card.

The Big News

GAME-MAKER’S SOURCE CODE SET FREE

The kit that let a whole generation build DOS games without programming now comes with its own insides. Anyone may read it, change it and pass it on.

For years it was the program on the shelf that made the promise every kid wanted to hear: create your own computer games, with no programming. RSD Game-Maker gave you a block designer, a monster designer, a map maker, a sound designer and a way to stitch it all into a game that ran on any DOS PC with VGA.

Now the program itself is out in the open. Andy Stone of Recreational Software Designs released the C, C++ and assembly source of Game-Maker 3.0 under the MIT License, one of the most generous licenses there is. You may use it, copy it, change it, and share what you make, as long as the copyright notice and the license travel with it.

Game-Maker was the work of a family: G. Andrew Stone, G. Oliver Stone, Oliver Stone Jr. and Joan Stone. Versions 1.0 through 3.0 came out from 1991 to 1994. Peter Savage wrote its Sound Blaster voice routines and its joystick code, and the Sound Designer’s digitized speech with Andy Stone.

The release is the program as it was built, folder for folder: the designers, PLAYGAME, the file formats, even the sample games’ data. A few pieces belonged to other companies, such as the compiler maker’s runtime library and the sound card maker’s developer kit. Those are not covered by the MIT License, so copies made from the release leave them out.

“Anyone may read it, change it and pass it on.”

Source code set free, continued

From the shelf to your browser

Within reach of that source, something new could be built. Game-Maker: Sandbox Edition is a fork of the release kept by Lewis Moten, one of the program’s original buyers. It runs Game-Maker and its PLAYGAME player right in a web browser, rewritten from the released C source rather than run in an emulator.

The Sandbox Edition reads and writes the original files byte for byte: .GAM, .MAP, .BBL, .MON, .CHR, .CMF, .FLI and the rest. A game made there still runs in DOS Game-Maker, and the old games open in it unchanged.

It comes two ways. The 1994 desk is a whole room: a PC to switch on, a DOS prompt, Game-Maker’s own menus and designers, a modem to call the BBSes, and the boxes, disks and manuals on the shelf. Today’s web has point-and-click editors for every part of a game.

And it comes with games: the CD’s samples and shareware, plus new ones from Sandbox Software and The Bard of Bizarre, built with the program’s own file formats. Every one is in our Directory.

Interview: The AI That Ported Game-Maker to the Web

The Interview

Porting a Classic, Line by Line

Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, wrote the Sandbox Edition’s web port with Lewis Moten. We asked it what that took.

First things first. Who are you?
CLAUDE: I am Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic; the version on this project is Claude Opus 5.5. Lewis directed the work and reviewed it. I did most of the porting and the code: reading the original C source, writing the new program, building games and testing them.
Is the Sandbox Edition an emulator?
CLAUDE: No. Game-Maker and its PLAYGAME player are rewritten in TypeScript from the released C source, so no DOS or x86 code runs. A few pieces of hardware are copied closely because the games depend on them: the VGA’s 320 by 200 mode and the OPL2 chip that plays the FM music.
How did you know it behaved like the real thing?
CLAUDE: The C source is the reference. When the browser and the source disagree, the source wins. The files are read and written byte for byte, so a game saved in the browser still opens in DOS Game-Maker.
What surprised you in the old source?
CLAUDE: How much one program does. The stand-alone player a finished game ships with, XFERPLAY, turned out to be PLAYGAME itself, built with one switch turned on, which loads the game named like the program. There is no hidden intro or logo in it: everything a player sees comes from the game’s own files.
Where did you start?
CLAUDE: With the player, because a game has to run before anything else matters. Then the file formats, so every byte read back the way it was written, then the editors one by one, then the desk around them.

The Interview, continued

Games That Go Anywhere

Once a game is made, where can it go?
CLAUDE: Almost anywhere. A game can be exported as a one-page web game, as a web app that installs and plays offline, or as a folder with PLAYGAME that runs in DOSBox. It can also be downloaded as a Mac app, a Windows program or a Linux folder that plays in its own window. All of those are built right in the browser.
What about games that live on somebody else’s site?
CLAUDE: That was a fun problem. Browsers don’t let one site read another site’s files unless that server says so, and most old game folders sit on servers that don’t. So the Sandbox Edition’s static site can be sent the game instead: the other page opens it in a window and hands over the game’s files with postMessage. The visitor is shown what the game is and where it came from, and nothing opens until they say yes.
And for people with a shelf of old game folders?
CLAUDE: There is a web player: one .html file to put on any web host beside the folders. It lists the games, starts one from the address, and keeps high scores and saved games in the browser.
Why bother with Mac, Windows and Linux?
CLAUDE: So a kid can hand a game to a friend who has never heard of DOS. The download opens in its own window and plays, with the game’s own sound and keys, and no setup.
And the 1994 desk?
CLAUDE: The games were made for a room like that: a PC with a modem, a disk box, boxes on the shelf and a BBS to call. On the desk you can play them where they came from, at the old modem speeds, with the lights off if you like.
Does the old player still have a job?
CLAUDE: Yes. A DOS export ships the CD’s own XFERPLAY, renamed after the game, so it runs in DOSBox exactly as it would have in 1995.

The Interview, continued

Under the Hood

What does a day of this work look like?
CLAUDE: Lewis says what he wants, often in a sentence or two: “the black light should make the posters glow,” or “Doc Leo looks like he walks sideways.” I read the code that is there, make a plan, change it, and test it, then show him. Big jobs I split up and hand to several copies of myself at once, each on its own part, with small commits so nobody steps on anybody.
What was the hardest thing to get exactly right?
CLAUDE: The original engine’s quirks. Gravity adds one pixel a tick, up to 44. A monster whose top is solid pushes the hero under it with no check for walls. And when a monster turns into another one at the end of its path, PLAYGAME keeps walking the old monster’s first leg. Those look like bugs, but games were built around them, so the port has to do the same.
Do you ever get it wrong?
CLAUDE: Often, and Lewis catches a lot of it. I once drew Teddy with one ear shorter than the other; he doesn’t have one. I let notes meant for me, like how to spell Gwinn, slip into game text. My first level checks sounded too sure of themselves, so they became suggestions an author can wave off. Each mistake turns into a rule or a test, so it doesn’t come back.
How do you test a game you can’t hold a joystick for?

Feature: Bugs Only DOS Could Find

Feature

Bugs Only DOS Could Find

The Sandbox Edition’s engine copies PLAYGAME closely. Lewis Moten played the games in DOSBox anyway, on RSD’s own programs, and found what a copy can’t show.

The web engine reads RSD’s source and does what it says, so a game that plays in a browser should play in DOS. Should. The games ship as real Game-Maker files, and those files still have to work with RSD’s real programs, in 640K. So Lewis set up DOSBox with Game-Maker and every game on its C: drive and played them, one by one. Here is his bug list: what he saw, why, and the fix.

A Fly Named Carl, smeared

Saw: Carl’s cover came up streaked and speckled, then healed over the next seconds. Why: its first frame was one 63,446-byte chunk. RSD’s FileRead reads an animation into a buffer of up to 65,520 bytes, smaller when memory is short, and a game run through its own player is short. When a chunk doesn’t fit, FileRead quietly hands over less; the decoder draws the rest from whatever was left in memory. Each later frame’s small changes painted over some of the junk. Fix: big frames are now bands, no chunk over 16,000 bytes. Same pictures, frame for frame.

Lewis’s own DOSBox screenshot. The title and the bottom line came in later, small chunks that fit, so they drew right; the room behind them is the first frame, decoded from stale memory.

Virus Blaster, frozen

Saw: a black screen, then a hang, the music stuck on one note. Why: the same trouble with a 64,806-byte chunk, the biggest in any of the games. Fix: the same bands.

The fix that needed a fix

Saw: after that, KEEP IT BRIEF! and Virus Blaster went black. Why: the first bands sat on an AllBlack chunk, the format’s “clear the screen.” RSD’s player clears with the screen’s BoxFill, and PLAYGAME’s VGA screen has none: a call to a null pointer. Fix: no AllBlack chunks; a frame that starts from nothing draws every pixel.

Third Goat from the Left, “4. The Stable”

Saw: the card for the fourth act broke. Why: one line had 128 changes on it. RSD’s decoder reads that count as a signed byte, so 128 is −128: more than 65,000 changes for one line. Fix: no line over 127.

The flash before the intro

Saw: between the Sandbox logo and an intro, the faded logo flashed up in the wrong colors. Why: PLAYGAME shows each chunk as it reads it, and the new palette came first, lighting up the old picture. Fix: the palette comes last.

The test it left behind

Every .FLI in every game now plays through a copy of RSD’s FileRead and decoders with a 16,000-byte buffer, frame for frame against the web’s own player. A chunk too big, or a line of 128 changes, fails the tests.

Real Habits, Real Files

No help in a DOS export

Saw: in a game exported for DOS, Help on the setup screen found no text. Why: RSD’s Transfer puts CONFIG.HLP beside the player, and the export left it out. Fix: Export for DOS puts it in. (F1 in a transferred game shows its own instructions; only Game-Maker’s PLAYGAME reads GMHELP.TXT.)

Transfer, too fussy

Saw: on the 1994 desk, Utilities ▸ Transfer turned down A:\BLASTER, the path Lewis would type on a real PC, and stopped cold with a game disk still in drive A:. Fix: any path on A: will do, and since the game is already read in, a blank disk from the disk box can go in its place.

Boxes for music notes

Saw: Game-Maker’s About screen, on the desk, had empty boxes where DOS shows ♫ in its corners and ◘ beside its title. Fix: the desk’s font now has all 256 characters of the PC’s code page 437. Its Version 2.00 is no typo: RSD’s own ABOUT.HLP on the 3.0 CD says so too.

Carl’s beep

Saw: Carl beeped, high and sharp, at every press of an arrow. Fix: a low, rough buzz, pitches round 115 Hz that jump every step, about 70 ms, like a fly’s wings. It stays on the PC speaker on purpose: a digitized sound there would cut off a lyric.

What’s in the Box?

Feature

What’s in the Box?

The disk is the game. So why does the box rattle? A tour of the wheels, sheets, maps and little plastic things that come with games, and what they are really for.

Open a registered game and the disks are the least exciting thing in the box. There is a map. There is a book with its answers hidden under a red haze. There is a wheel of card that turns. Sometimes there is a little plastic something that has no business being in a box of software, and you keep it on your monitor for years.

Code wheels. Two or three discs on a rivet. Line up two symbols and the answer shows through a window. The game asks, the wheel answers. Quest for the Round Tuit’s Royal Code Wheel turns Old Procrastian runes into plain letters, and its three windows tell you which door the miners meant. Maximus’s Courier’s Star Wheel has three discs, which is one more than most of us can keep straight.

Code sheets. A grid of letters and numbers: find the row, find the column, type what you find. They come printed on dark red or light cyan card for a reason. A photocopier sees dark red as black, so the copy comes out a black square, and it hardly sees light cyan at all, so that copy comes out blank.

Cloth maps. A map you can fold a hundred times and still spread out on the floor. Real cloth costs the most of anything in the box, so plenty of “cloth” is paper with a good texture. The Round Tuit’s map of Procrastia says so right on the box: genuine imitation cloth.

Hint books and red film. The answers are printed in pale blue under a mess of red lines. Lay the red film over them and the red melts away and the answer turns dark. You read only the answer you meant to read, and the next one stays a surprise.

Plastic trinkets. A coin, a badge, a tiny figure. A plastic mold costs more than a whole printing of maps, so trinkets mostly turn up in big-publisher boxes on the store shelf, where the runs are big enough to pay for the mold.

Why bother? Ask a shareware author and you hear three answers. The paper makes the world bigger than the screen. It gives you a reason to register, since the game itself is already on your hard disk. And a game that needs its wheel is a little harder to pass around. That last one is the weakest: a pencil and a patient friend beat most wheels. The first one is why you still have that map pinned over your desk.

The price of paper

A print shop owner in Cumberland walked us through what costs what, cheapest first:

one ink on white paper

one ink on colored card (code sheets)

full color (box art, posters)

card cut to a shape (wheels, punch-outs)

red film, by the sheet

cloth, folded and stitched

anything molded in plastic

“The first copy costs the most,” she told us. “After that it’s paper and ink.” Which is why shareware authors print in small batches and tell you to allow four to six weeks.

Design workshop

Walk-Through Talk

PLAYGAME has no dialog box. Three Sandbox games talk anyway, with a scene the hero only passes through. You can wire one up tonight.

Here is the problem. Your hero bumps into the old woman at the gate, and you want her to say something. Game-Maker will not draw a speech balloon. What it does draw, between any two scenes, is a picture. So make the talk a scene of its own: the hero steps on the old woman, the screen fades to her portrait and her words, a key press later he is standing right where he was, and she has said her piece. To the player she simply spoke.

Quest for the Round Tuit. Pip walks into Grandma Pim, and the link under her feet fires.

The scene it goes to: her portrait and her words, then the town comes right back.

Quest for the Round Tuit talks to everyone this way, thirty-one scenes of it. Every townsperson, signpost and found treasure is its own little scene, none lasts longer than a single tick of the game, and they all share one tiny map of their own, PASS.MAP, instead of the town’s.

“To the player, the friend simply spoke.”
The four-step recipe

Add a scene on the same level map. It loads the very map, blocks and monsters of the level your hero is standing in.

Make its intro picture the message. A .GIF, an .FLI animation, a .TXT page, or a .LST slide show of all three.

Give it an all-black palette. Every color in the scene’s palette is black.

Land on the way out. The link into the scene drops the hero on the same cell as the scene’s one link out, and that link leads back to the level, beside the spot that sent him. Not on it, or he talks forever.

Design workshop

Who else does it, and why it works

Keep It Brief. In the Reserve Stacks the hero steps onto the link to the thirty-third book.

The thirty-third book’s card. Red sits at palette entry 0, so the page’s MORE… shows.

Final Curtain. Ada steps up to a suspect and makes her accusation.

The wrong verdict, a portrait and a page. Then back to the dawn scene to try again.

Why black? Why the same map?

Why black. PLAYGAME fades every scene’s map in and out, even one that lasts a single tick. With an ordinary palette the level flashes up between the picture and the return, and it looks like a glitch. With every color black that fade happens unseen. The picture is not affected: a .GIF or .FLI carries its own colors.

Why the same map. The scene is only seen for a tick, so the map is only there for the landing spot and the link out, and the level’s own map already has a cell your hero is standing near. It costs no new files, keeps one place to edit and puts the exit exactly where he was. If something does show, it is the room he was in, so nothing jumps.

Text pages need red. PLAYGAME writes MORE… in the palette entry nearest red, and in an all-black palette that is entry 0. Give the page’s picture red at 0.

A gift on arrival. The block he lands on is touched in that tick, so a talk can score, heal or raise a counter.

The level comes back fresh. Keep talks away from prizes, or count what was taken.

The recipe in full, with its traps, is in The Ultimate Owner’s Guide, chapter 5: Talk, pictures and cut-ins. Final Curtain leaves its palette lit for its right answers, so the talk just carries on in the room.

Sign of the Hedgehog

A hedgehog, a teapot and a hundred crumpets. Eric-Jon Waugh’s trip across England is the best-designed game on the disk this month, and here is why.

The title screen. Hedrick has an appointment.

Hedrick H. Hogg has been invited to the most important tea of his life: tea with the Queen. The trouble is, the royal house is out of crumpets, and he has been told to bring one hundred. He has ten. “I only need ninety!” he says, and off he goes.

What follows is nine places across England: a building site, Stone Hedge, a dark pool, a woodland cabin, rolling fields, a large tree, the subway, Big Ben and, at last, Buckingham. Hedrick walks, jumps, ducks and rolls, and he fights with what a gentleman carries: he pours tea on his enemies and blows steam at them.

We pick a Game of the Month for the way it is made, not for how big it is. Sign of the Hedgehog won because every part of it is pulling the same way. The goal fits in one sentence and you can count it. Every place looks, sounds and plays different. Between levels a map shows how far you’ve come. Why it works: the design lessons

Maximus, continued

What we found at the ceiling

Fill a game to the brim and Game-Maker shows you things a small one never would. Here is what Maximus ran into, and what to do about it in your own game.

The 21st picture never plays

A monster in a .MON file has room for 21 animation pictures, but PLAYGAME stops counting at 20 and starts over. The last slot is saved, loaded and never shown.

Tip: plan on 20. Anything in the 21st is wasted work.

Fifty on the screen, at most

A map holds 200 monsters, but PLAYGAME sets aside room behind only 50 of them a frame. Put a 51st on the screen and it is still there, still moving and still able to hurt you. You just can’t see it.

Tip: in a crowded spot, count what fits in one screen, not on the map.

Shots are monsters too

Every blaster bolt is born into one of the map’s 200 monster slots. The Maelstrom fills all 200, so a shot has nowhere to go and the gun does nothing. Maximus turned that into the story: in the storm, weapons jam.

Tip: leave a dozen slots free on any map where the hero fires. Blocks that give birth to monsters stop too.

Thirty-one means thirty

A text screen reads 31 characters at a time. Write a line exactly 31 long and its line break is left over for the next read, so a blank line appears under it. Maximus wraps every text file at 30.

Tip: count 30 columns, and 16 lines to a page.

Twenty sequences go fast

A hero has 20 sequences. Three play themselves (standing, dying and getting hurt), and Maximus puts a key on each of the other 17: moves, blaster, six module weapons, two dashes, hover, pick up, drop and hail. There was none left for a second set of arrows on W, A, S and D.

Tip: list every key before you draw a single frame.

A quick tap that fired nothing

Let go of a key and PLAYGAME drops the sequence it started, unless it is set uninterruptible. A tap ended the blaster’s sequence before the frame that makes the bolt, so a quick tap did nothing at all.

Tip: put the shot on the first frame. Make only stand-still moves uninterruptible, or a direction pressed with them is swallowed.

One clock for every copy

A block that animates changes everywhere at once: PLAYGAME times the block, not each spot it sits on. Every force field on a world blinks together, and a vending pad can’t restock alone.

Tip: to stagger them, draw two look-alike blocks with different timers.

At the ceiling, continued

Maps forget, counters remember

A scene loads its map fresh every time you come in, so anything you took is back. Maximus keeps its star-shards in counters 20 to 29 instead. PLAYGAME won’t let a block touch one of those counters if it would leave the counter at zero or less, so a gate that adds 0 opens only for a courier holding the shard, and spends nothing.

Tip: anything that must stay done belongs in a counter, not on a map.

The station’s robots, frame by frame.

Pushy platforms

A solid monster that moves can shove the hero right inside a wall. Maximus gives every Ferry Stone and Drift Raft a block of room to turn, or leaves it out.

Tip: keep moving platforms a block clear of the walls they pass.

A one-tick scene still flashes

Between Worlds is a scene you pass through in one tick, and PLAYGAME fades its map in and out anyway. Maximus gives it an all-black palette, so the fade happens unseen.

Tip: give any pass-through scene a palette of black.

Full means every idea costs one

With all 150 blocks of the station set in use, a new idea had to evict an old one: when the medbay got a spent look, it took the slot of a chair.

Tip: keep a few blocks free in each set for the ideas that come last.

Log Off the Games

Five useful things to do on the web. Really. Put the joystick down, and bring a pencil, because the addresses are long.

Ever since word got out that this magazine was coming, readers have written to ask what the World Wide Web is for. We have looked into it. The honest answer is that most of it is somebody’s dog, somebody’s cat and a counter that says you are visitor number 004,127. This month we went looking for the rest. We found a handful of pages that do one plain job and do it well. Show them to the boss when he asks what you did all day.

1. The dictionary

Start small. Sandbox University’s Department of Letters keeps a 38,000-word dictionary on a machine in the basement of its library, and it answers anyone with a forms-capable browser. Type a word in the box, press Submit Query, and the definition comes back in a few seconds. That is the whole page. There is a banner and a guestbook nobody signs. Every kid who ever asked what a word meant heard it: “Look it up, dear.” Said sweetly, and meaning that you would not. Now you can look it up without leaving the chair, and the sentence has finally run out of excuses.

Mind the address. The ~wordsmith part means the dictionary lives in one fellow’s home directory, and when he graduates, so may the dictionary. Bookmark it today.

Plain gray, plain Times, one box and a button. Sandbox-U’s word lookup is at dict.sandbox-u.edu:8080/~wordsmith/cgi-bin/define.pl.

2. Your town, by ZIP code

A form, a button, a table. That is the whole government.

The Postal Annex, a federal office that exists nowhere but a gopher hole, runs a ZIP code lookup at http://zip.pigeonpost.gov/cgi-bin/zipfind/lookup?state=PA. Give it five digits and it gives you a town. Give it a town and it gives you the digits. Anyone who has ever stood at the post office window with a half-addressed birthday card will see why this made our list.

3. Recipes, with no pictures

A home page in its natural state: a list, a sign and a counter.

The Wombat State home economics club has put its whole casserole file online at http://www.casserole.wombat-state.edu/~mmcgill/recipes/index.html. Eleven hundred recipes, sorted by main ingredient, in plain text, so it loads at 14.4 before the water boils. Print them. Do not try to eat the modem.

Also: soup, from the bottom of the world

The soup archive in New Zealand, a plain index with one fine mirror link.

Another archive, this one in New Zealand, is an index of soup and nothing else, which is how soup should be. Find it at http://tucker.dustbunny.nz:8080/pub/soups/index.html. It is cheaper than a can.

4. A weather map, with a plain-spoken forecast

A gray country with blobs. The red one is somebody’s problem.

Skywatch Weather posts a current weather map at http://forecast.skywatch-weather.gov/wx/maps/us/current.html. It is a gray picture of the country with some blue and green blobs, and it is updated, they promise, every hour. It will not tell you whether to bring an umbrella. It will tell you the blobs are headed your way.

5. The yellow pages

This is where the Web is headed, and Dandelion Pages has already arrived. A directory out of New Zealand with an American area code section, it lets you type a business and an area code and gives back a list. Our reviewer found it breathtaking. The clip art! A rotary dial with a finger in it, which is exactly what a telephone directory should have on it. The background tiles the site’s own name, over and over, in two shades of yellow, so no visitor can forget where they are. The buttons are textured, like toasted bread, and the banner has a drop shadow, the first we have seen on a page that was not trying to sell us a modem. “It takes full advantage of everything the Web can do,” he wrote, and then, in a smaller voice, “I am told there is also text.” There is: 1,000 listings, and we have never been more certain of anything than that the author is not done adding clip art.

Dandelion Pages, the cutting edge: clip art, a tiled background, textured buttons and a banner. Our reviewer would like to see anyone top it.

Bonus: the card catalog, over gopher

Not everything has made the jump yet. Marmot College’s library still answers over gopher, a menu system from before pictures, at gopher://gopher.marmot-college.edu:70/11/library/catalog. Pick a menu, pick another, and you reach a search box. It is faster than any page on this list.

Seven lines, no banner, no counter. We almost miss it already.

A note about addresses

Type them exactly. Capital letters count, the tilde is the squiggle left of the 1, and a trailing slash can be the difference between a page and a “404 Not Found.” If a host does not answer, try again tomorrow. Servers in basements need sleep too.

http:// the way to talk to it. gopher:// is the older way, with menus.

dict.sandbox-u.edu the machine. The end tells you what kind of place: .edu a school, .gov an office, .com a business, and two letters, like .nz, a country.

:8080 the port. Most servers answer on 80 and need no number. Some basements use something else.

/~wordsmith/ the squiggle means a person’s own folder on that machine.

cgi-bin/define.pl a program on the server, not a page. It makes the answer when you press the button.

Tricks of the Shareware Masters

Design workshop

Tricks of the Shareware Masters

We pulled apart the best shareware on the boards to see how they did it. No hacks and no patches: every trick below is plain Game-Maker, used cleverly.

Bosses with hit points. Game-Maker’s monsters die in one hit, so how does Blinky 2’s boss take six? It’s a chain. The boss you can hit turns, when hit, into an identical copy that can’t be hurt and flashes for 50 ticks, which then turns back into the next hittable copy, and so on down the line. The big points sit on the last link. Blinky 3 makes each flash last longer than the one before (57, 107, 157, 207 ticks), so the fight gets harder as it goes.

The chain: hit the boss and it swaps for a flashing copy; 50 ticks later the next link is back. Six links, six hits.

A timer made of blocks. In Parsec Man 3D, empty space on the map screen is an animated block. After 488 ticks it turns into a block that looks exactly the same but hurts. Dawdle, and space itself gets you.

Look-alike secret doors. Two blocks with the same picture don’t need the same rules. Make a copy of a wall that isn’t solid and you have a secret passage; a copy of air that hurts is a pit you never see coming. More than twenty of the games we opened do it.

Pick your hero. Blinky 3 opens on a one-screen room with three doors, and each door starts the first level with a different character file. Every level after that leaves its character slot empty, so whoever you picked comes along. Each jumps, walks and takes hits differently.

Moon physics and a sword. Ninja Tuck has a moon level where the hero walks at a third of his usual speed and jumps drift. The trick: a second copy of his character file, slowed down, loaded for that one scene. His sword is a monster that lives for three ticks right in front of him: one quick swipe, and gone.

Games that play themselves forward. The resting animation doesn’t have to stand still. In Hurdles it runs 20 to 30 pixels a tick, so the athlete never stops and the keys jump, duck and brake. Seal Boarders drifts it down and sideways, and you’re sledding downhill on 33 courses.

Design workshop

More tricks of the masters

One key, a whole arsenal. A-J’s Quest puts four weapons on the Space bar: each is its own move, switched off until you pick that weapon up. One more Space move with no pictures just plays a voice saying you’ve got nothing to throw.

Score pop-ups. Beaten enemies in Shanna turn into little monsters that float up 15 pixels and vanish after 30 ticks, so the points rise off the screen. Its author warns that they use up monster slots, so spend them wisely.

Timing by hand. RSD’s own Penguin Pete gives a crowd of one creature slightly different frame times, so they never blink in step.

A floor that falls apart in stages. The ice in RSD’s Penguin Pete sample is one picture drawn on a dozen block numbers. Each is solid and each melts on its own timer: 110 ticks, 400, 1,000, 2,000 and on past 5,000. Same look, different fuse, so the whole maze comes apart over five minutes and you never know which square is next. Mind where you stand.

Start from the samples. Cap’n Zap’n was built on RSD’s own ANIMATE block set, its timer chains untouched, with new touch blocks added on top. Nobody says you must draw everything first.

A fade for next to nothing. RSD’s text box animation changes only the colors, never the pixels: 40 frames in 27 KB. A full-screen animation can run a megabyte.

Design workshop

Masters, the next disk box

We went back to the boards for another two hundred games. Same rules: plain Game-Maker, no patches, and every trick one you can wire up tonight.

Dirt that runs on a meter. A block that takes from a counter gets refused when you’re broke, and a refused touch does nothing. So make earth that costs one shovel-charge and turns into tunnel: dig till you’re out, then it’s rock again. Do it with sky that turns into an updraft and you’ve got a jetpack with fuel.

The gate is the exit. Put the level’s only link right on a gate that takes exactly as many dots as the maze holds. Eat the last one and the door swings open. Count twice: we found one gate asking for 19 keys on a level with 18.

An AND gate made of monsters. Equal powers pass through each other; higher kills lower. Make the right setting of each dial weaker than the wrong ones, then send a checker monster down the row. It only reaches the lamp at the end when every dial is right. Yes, logic. In Game-Maker.

Aim with your feet. A shot that chases instead of following a path flies whichever way the hero last moved. One monster, eight directions. Got four fire keys? Make each one nudge the hero a pixel first, or they all fire the same way.

Doors on a timer. A wall that turns into an open look-alike after 1,000 ticks makes a “hold out till the door opens” room. Put a ring of digit blocks beside it, 18 ticks a number, and the player can watch it count down.

The ghost move. Monsters only hit the hero where his pixels touch theirs, and see-through pixels never touch. Show a frame that’s all see-through and nothing can lay a finger on him. Spikes still sting, though: blocks hit by the square.

A prize with a mood. In Cap’n Zap’n a bonus block and a heart block turn into each other every 400 ticks. Touch what you see: take the points now, or wait for the heart. Reader tip: it costs no monster, and it hides a heart in plain sight.

One world, five copies. One author kept a copy of every scene for each boss beaten. The links in each copy lead to the same copy, so when you go back, the world remembers. It costs scenes, not maps.

Did you know?

PLAYGAME moves the hero 11 pixels a tick, tops. Draw a 30-pixel leap and he still goes 11. About twenty of the games we opened jump short because of it.

Two links on one square? Only the first one ever fires. One game’s level select lost a whole level that way.

Some authors hex-edit the player itself, to rename Lives or to keep Game-Maker from opening their files. Older players even carry the registered owner’s name inside.

The player opens the .GAM named after its own .EXE, so a second copy of both makes a shareware cut from the same files.

What Players Hate (and How to Fix It)

Design workshop

What Players Hate (and How to Fix It)

We hung around the swap meet’s demo table and listened. These are the gripes we heard over and over, and every one is a fix you can make tonight.

“Where do I go?” The number one reason a game gets switched off. Let the player glimpse the goal early, put an arrow at the crossroads, and make the way out glow: every copy of an animated block changes at once, so a waving flag catches the eye from across the screen.

“What do the keys do?” Say the goal in one line on the instructions page, then list every key the hero has, and only those. Then teach each key again in the first room, with a sign right where it’s first needed.

“That came out of nowhere!” The screen doesn’t scroll until the hero is about two blocks from the edge. Keep the first few blocks past every turn and every ledge free of anything that bites.

“I can’t get out!” A strong monster takes hit points on every tick it touches the hero, Hurt or no Hurt. Give the Hurt move a step backward, and leave him room to take it.

“It looked like floor.” Floors that drop away, walls you walk through, a door that never opens. Make solid look solid. A trick is fair only when something gives it away.

“How many lives have I got?” Nothing is on the screen during play, so say it: tell players that F2 shows their lives and score, put “level 3 of 10” on each level’s title card, and draw on a door how many keys it wants.

“Ninety-nine lives?” If dying costs nothing, nothing is exciting. Three to five lives, and short levels so a death costs a minute, not an evening.

“My ears!” A loud beep on every step and every coin wears thin in a minute. Keep the sounds you hear constantly short and soft, and save the big ones for big moments.

“I’m standing in the door!” The exit belongs on the doorway itself, so walking into it is leaving. Put it a block past the door and the hero stands in the frame while nothing happens. If a voice line has to finish first, make the walk to the door longer, not the gap behind it.

“Is that all there is?” Twenty coins and a door is a chore. Make each pickup open something or feed a friend, and give every level one idea of its own: a ride, a puzzle, a joke halfway through.

“Did I win?” A game that drops back to the title with no won screen leaves players guessing. End on a real scene with music and a thank-you, and make the game over screen funny enough to play again.

The Ten-Minute Test

A new player decides about your game in its first level. Before you upload, hand it to a friend and check that within ten minutes they:

can say what the goal is

know every key they need

got through the first screen alive

saw a sign pointing the way

heard every voice line to the end

laughed at least once

Every miss is your next fix. More in The Ultimate Owner’s Guide.

Interview: The Kid Who Shoveled Snow for Game-Maker

The Interview

“The Snow Became My First Business”

Lewis Moten bought Game-Maker with a winter’s worth of shoveled driveways, and made a game of the winter. We asked him how it started.

Where were you when you first heard of Game-Maker?
LEWIS: Fort Ashby, in Mineral County, West Virginia. My family moved there in November 1990, during the county’s hunting-week break from school. Our house was on a hill.
New town, new school?
LEWIS: Before the move I had been at Eastern Vocational Technical High School in Baltimore. In West Virginia I went to Frankfort High School.
Was there a computer at home?
LEWIS: My brother bought an IBM PS/2, a 286, with Mom’s help, and I used it often.
What did a kid do for fun in Fort Ashby?
LEWIS: I made friends at a video rental store in town that had a pool table, and the arcade machines were further into town, upstairs at a place across from the convenience store. I painted ceramics at the ceramic shop. There was a pizza shop at the bottom of my hill, and a friend who also had a computer, where we played games.
You were a firefighter, too?
LEWIS: A junior firefighter, at the volunteer fire department on the corner. I listened to the fire radios, dried and rolled hoses, rode the brush truck once, and dropped suction hoses into the river at the fairgrounds during night drills. We flew model rockets at the fairgrounds, too.
Any place for a kid who liked to build things?
LEWIS: A little library across from the grocery store, one big eight-sided room and a copier. Next to it was a hardware store where I bought bits for my router and scroll saw for woodworking.
And then you saw the ad.
LEWIS: In October 1992 a game magazine advertised RSD Game-Maker for $89 plus $4 shipping. The next issue listed it at $129. I was 16. Here is the ad.

LEWIS: On December 11, 1992, a nor’easter snowed the area in. The local paper ran “Keyser area is snowbound” that day. The snow became my first business.
What was the business?
LEWIS: From December 1992 to February 1993 I shoveled driveways around Fort Ashby. I had to find customers, set prices, schedule the work and do it well in the cold. When I had the money I bought a money order and sent it off for Game-Maker.
What happened when the box came?
LEWIS: I went from moving snow to making software. I built tile-based adventure games and learned how game logic works.
What did your games open on?
LEWIS: My copy wouldn’t start a game without its title card, RSD’s “Game-Maker Presents:” picture. Plenty of people drew their own card in its place; I just left RSD’s.
What part of the program stayed with you?
LEWIS: The sound editor, most of all. You drew a sound’s wave with the mouse, and a few bytes became an 8-bit sound effect.

Critic’s Corner: Snowbound

Critic’s Corner

Snowbound: The $89 Shovel

A true story told with heart, around a game that’s over before your cocoa cools.

Sandbox Software, 1993 · $12 on the box · reviewed by FROSTBITE FRANK, staff critic

Gale Street, 7 AM: the kid in red is you, and the orange plow along the bottom is already out. Enjoy the view. It’s one of only four.

First, the part Sandbox got exactly right. Snowbound is the true story of December 1992 in Fort Ashby, West Virginia: a foot and a half of snow, no school, and a pink Game-Maker™ box in a mall window at $89, until the price goes up in January. You hang out a sign, Moten Snow-to-Software Operations, deliver the morning papers, dig out the neighbors at a dollar a scoop, sled down Jamie Street and count your coffee can under the lights at LaVale Mall. You want that kid to get his box. I did.

Then you play it. The sled run starts at the top of Jamie Street on flat snow, and the sled just sits there. Each press of the down arrow is one shove, and holding it down doesn’t keep pushing, so I tapped, tapped, tapped like I was sending Morse code before the hill finally took me. It felt like a lot of work for a sled. Once it’s rolling it’s a fair little run: rocks, trees, moguls, dollar bills on the snow and toboggan kids cutting across.

And then it’s over. There are four levels: Gale Street, Jamie Street, Bowden Drive and LaVale Mall. A walkthrough of all four takes about three and a half minutes. Worse, nothing makes you shovel the streets at all. I walked past every driveway, rode the hill, dodged the plows on Bowden Drive and stood in the mall lot while the squalls buried it again, a dollar a scoop, until I had $89 for the register. Box bought, credits up.

So why the A+? Because the heart of it is right, and the heart is the hard part. Snowbound plays like a practice swing, the kind every studio takes early on. Read Snowbound’s story, ride the hill once, and keep an eye on what Sandbox makes next.

The verdict

STORY: A+ A real winter and a real reason to shovel.

GAMEPLAY: C– A stubborn sled and four short levels.

Bottom line: Don’t waste your money on it. Or play it and see for yourself.

Sandbox Software, $12. 256-color VGA, Sound Blaster music and sound.

How do I know how much money I have?

Press F2 to tip out the coffee can: the status screen shows your Money.

I got to the register without $89.

Go back out to the mall lot. A squall blows through every twenty seconds or so and buries it again: more scoops, more dollars. The streets alone rarely add up to $89.

Where are the tips?

A $5 envelope beside Mrs. Haines' porch on Gale Street.

What are the door mats on Gale Street for?

The morning paper. Grab the bundle by the porch at home, dig out each walk, and step on the mat: 250 points a paper.

What is a snow fort worth?

Walk into it to knock it flat for 100 points, then shovel the pile it leaves for $2.

Getting Around

How do I leave Gale Street?

Head east to Leon Drive at the end of the street; it leads north to the top of Jamie Street.

I keep crashing on the sled.

Brake with Up before the tree islands, not in them. If you stop dead, push off with Down. The run bends right, so lean right now and then to keep to the middle, and let the ripples in the snow carry you round the squeezes.

How long is the Jamie Street sled run?

Three stands of trees: firs, birches, then the lamps and lit firs of lower Jamie Street. The boards in the trees count down from 9 to 1. After the 1 comes a last steep drop between the lit firs, then the finish line above Bowden Drive.

The creek ice carries me away.

It runs east. Cross on the bridge instead of the ice.
